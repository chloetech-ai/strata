Space against speed on a reordered index

On the clustered collection the 500 queries average 2.8 terms and touch 576,000 postings each; a conjunction returns 309 documents and a disjunction 563,000. The charts plot each codec's size on the query lists against its fastest algorithm; blue points are Pareto-optimal (nothing else is both smaller and faster).

  • The AND frontier has three members. It runs from interpolative coding (4.90 bits per posting, 0.14 ms) through PEF (4.94 bits, 0.035 ms) to Roaring (9.32 bits, 0.018 ms). PEF is within 1% of interpolative coding's size but 3.8× faster, because its NextGEQ is a select over the high bits instead of a block decode.
  • Roaring buys speed with space. It is 2.0× faster than PEF for AND and 5.8× faster for OR (0.38 against 2.21 ms), at 1.9× the size. Pibiri & Venturini report 4–8× (AND) and 14–18× (OR) for CRoaring over list codecs at about twice the space; the direction holds here, with smaller gaps on these skewed queries.
  • Block codecs sit in the middle. BP128 and the bitpacking crate take 0.051–0.065 ms at 7.69 bits, and OptPFor is about as fast at 5.92 bits, 23% smaller. VByte is 1.4× slower than BP128 with no space advantage. Uncompressed arrays (0.027 ms) beat every compressed list codec, PEF by 1.3×, at 6.5× its size.
  • Unions are bound by their output. Every list codec needs 1.8–3.5 ms, that is 3.2–6.2 ns per output docID, whatever its decoding speed. Only representations that OR whole machine words, or need no decoding, escape this: Roaring (0.38 ms, about 0.7 ns per result, or 0.23 ms if it only counts), the hybrid index (1.3 ms) and uncompressed arrays (1.32 ms with a pairwise merge, 1.73 ms through a bitmap).
  • Implementation matters as much as format. With identical containers and size, this crate's Roaring answers AND 3.0× faster than the roaring crate and OR 6.5× faster when docIDs are written out. When both only count, the OR gap shrinks to 2.7×, so most of the crate's union cost is its iterator. In the other direction, the bitpacking crate decodes BP128 blocks faster than my auto-vectorised unpacker (0.30 against 0.76 ns per posting).

Skipping beats decoding for conjunctions

The tables below give every algorithm's time on every codec (clustered collection; blue marks the fastest per row, not counting the count-only variants).

  • Rarest-first leapfrog is the best cursor conjunction for every codec. Driving the shortest list and calling NextGEQ on the others is 3.7–17× faster than decoding all lists and intersecting the arrays (43× for the roaring crate, whose decoding goes through a slow iterator). The gap is largest for the tries, PEF and Elias-Fano (15–17×), whose decoding is slowest relative to a jump.
  • Round-robin order costs 1.2–1.7×. Leapfrog Triejoin's round-robin seeks waste work when one list is much shorter than the rest, as here; it only won on two-term queries, and there by under 1%.
  • SIMD loses on real query shapes. On uncompressed arrays, SvS with galloping (0.029 ms) beats SvS with either NEON kernel by 7.7–9.5× (0.23–0.28 ms), although the 8×8 kernel wins by 6.8× on equal-size lists. The size ratios of real queries are large, so the adaptive kernel (0.027 ms, the fastest here) is the safe default. Small Adaptive (0.042 ms) and Sequential (0.052 ms) never beat SvS, which is Barbay et al.'s experimental finding.
  • Union algorithms: Lucene's bitset window or PISA's min-scan is the fastest cursor union for every codec, 1.2–1.6× faster than a heap of cursors. Native operations beat any cursor union for this crate's structures: 6.2× for Roaring and 2.4× for the hybrid index. The roaring crate's native union is 18% slower than a bitset window over its cursor.

What docID clustering buys

The uniform collection has exactly the same list lengths as the clustered one, but random docIDs. Comparing the two isolates the effect of docID order, which real systems control by reordering documents (by URL, or by recursive graph bisection).

  • Codecs that model local density gain the most. On the query lists, clustering cuts Run-Trie by 50%, Trie by 46%, interpolative by 42%, PEF by 39%, OptPFor by 33% and Roaring by 32%. BP128 gains 18% and VByte only 12%, because one wide gap still forces a wide block or extra bytes.
  • Plain Elias-Fano gains nothing (8.08 → 8.13 bits): its size depends only on \(u\) and \(n\), exactly as the formula says. On uniform data it ties PEF as the smallest codec, which is the case partitioning cannot improve.
  • Roaring and the tries pay most for randomness. At 13.2–13.7 bits per posting on uniform data they are 1.6–1.7× the size of PEF, because random docIDs leave few runs and many half-empty 16-bit arrays or trie paths.
  • Conjunctions get about 2× faster with clustering for every list codec (PEF 0.076 → 0.035 ms, BP128 0.145 → 0.065 ms), while Roaring changes little. Part of this comes from the workloads: the uniform queries touch 19% more postings and have smaller results, because random lists share fewer documents. The rest comes from skipping, since clustered lists put matching docIDs in fewer blocks and partitions.

Kernels: where merging stops paying

The pairwise kernels were timed on uncompressed random lists with a fixed short list of 10,000 docIDs and a long list 1 to 4,096 times larger. The chart plots nanoseconds per element of the short list, so a flat line means the cost tracks the short list (the \(m \log(n/m)\) regime) and a rising line means the long list is scanned (the \(m + n\) regime).

  • Similar sizes favour SIMD. At 1:1 the NEON 8×8 kernel takes 9.6 µs against 65 µs for the branchy merge, 6.8× faster. The NEON 4×4 kernel takes 24 µs, so even with 128-bit registers the 8-value block (two registers a side) is worth 2.5×. The branch-free merge (41 µs) beats the branchy one only at 1:1 and 1:2 and becomes the slowest kernel by 1:32, because its loop-carried dependency serialises every step.
  • Skew favours galloping. Galloping overtakes the 8×8 kernel between 1:32 and 1:64 and is 4.5× faster at 1:4,096 (4.5 ms against 19.9 ms). At 1:4,096 Baeza-Yates takes 1.3× galloping's time and plain binary search 3.6×; both lose badly when the sizes are similar (binary search needs 297 µs at 1:1).
  • The switch point is hardware-specific. The adaptive kernel uses CRoaring's 64× threshold. On the M2 that is too late: at 1:64 it pays 1.7× more than galloping (on the AVX-512 Xeon VM where this study first ran, galloping already won between 1:16 and 1:32). A threshold near 1:35 would track the lower envelope of both kernels, consistent with Baeza-Yates and Salinger's ~30:1 crossover for scalar code.

Longer queries

Each point uses 300 queries with exactly \(k\) co-occurring terms. As \(k\) grows from 2 to 6, the lists in a query hold more postings in total (400k to 740k–880k), yet every codec answers faster: the mean result shrinks from 744 to 2 documents, and the cost of a rarest-first conjunction follows the shortest list and the number of candidates it proposes, not the total length.

  • Roaring's lead narrows with more terms. It is 2.5× faster than PEF for two-term queries (0.019 against 0.046 ms) but only 1.6× faster at six (0.0069 against 0.011 ms). Selective conjunctions let cursor-based codecs take long NextGEQ jumps, while Roaring still visits every shared 2¹⁶ chunk, the effect Pibiri & Venturini describe.
  • Uncompressed arrays beat PEF at every length, by 1.3× at two terms and 2.2× at six, though they take more than six times the space.
  • Tries stay 2.8–3.3× behind PEF at every length. The likely reason is that the traversal pays a rank operation per trie level for every surviving region, which costs more than one cursor jump when a single list is very selective.

Real text: WikiText-103

WikiText-103 has 859,532 paragraphs, a 531,033-term vocabulary and 57 million postings. Its lists are far denser than the synthetic ones: function words occur in most paragraphs. The queries use terms with at least 100 postings and average 3.0 terms, with 64 documents per AND and 199,635 per OR.

  • Dense real lists suit Roaring. Its bitmap containers answer AND in 0.0043 ms, 3.8× faster than the fastest compressed list codec (PEF, 0.016 ms) and 2.1× faster than uncompressed arrays, and OR in 0.069 ms, 11× faster than any list codec with a cursor algorithm (0.75–1.3 ms) and 4.4× faster than the hybrid index. On the query lists it costs 8.47 bits per posting, 1.8× PEF.
  • The Elias-Fano family leads the list codecs: PEF and interpolative coding are the smallest (4.75 and 4.77 bits per posting on query lists), and Elias-Fano and PEF answer conjunctions 1.1–3.7× faster than the block codecs.
  • Small lists dominate whole-index space. Over all 531k lists the ranking changes: interpolative (8.46), Elias-Fano (8.51) and PEF (8.92) stay smallest, but the tries reach 23 bits per posting, because each of the many short lists pays for about 20 trie levels and their rank directories. Roaring pays a container header per list and lands at 15.4.
  • The OR algorithm matters as much as the codec. For every cursor codec, the Lucene-style bitset window is the fastest union, 1.4–2.0× faster than the heap.
